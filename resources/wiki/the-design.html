🧭 The design

A repository of its own

Sound is its own package, depending on neither Go Renderer nor Go UI:

  • A program without sound pays nothing. On Android, the output is C++ (Oboe) built with cgo; a program that plays nothing should not carry it.
  • It is useful beyond a window. A tool with no window, or a game drawn with another engine, can play sound with it.
  • It has its own life. An audio thread, suspending in the background, the phone’s audio focus.

The output: oto

ebitengine/oto sends the sound to each system: it is what the Ebitengine game engine plays through, and it is kept up to date.

System Through
Android Oboe, which oto carries and links statically (nothing more goes in the package): AAudio from Android 11, OpenSL ES before, because AAudio did not always say when the sound moved from the speaker to headphones
Linux PulseAudio, spoken in Go; ALSA when no PulseAudio runs, loaded when the program starts. No cgo
macOS AudioToolbox
Windows WASAPI
A web page An AudioWorklet, started at the first touch or key, as browsers ask
Nintendo 3DS The DSP, through libctru’s ndsp, built with TinyGo: Go Sound mixes the players itself and hands the DSP buffers of 16-bit stereo frames

Google advises Oboe or AAudio over OpenSL ES for new programs; Oboe makes that choice on each phone. Writing AAudio by hand (300 lines of C, as Go Renderer’s films are) would cover Android alone and lose Oboe’s fixes; miniaudio’s Go binding (malgo) needs cgo everywhere and has no web output.

Go Sound keeps oto behind two small interfaces (output and voice): the tests put a fake output there that pulls samples as the system would, and another output could take oto’s place.

How a sound plays

  1. A stream gives frames of samples, in its own rate and channels.
  2. The player converts them as they are read: channels to stereo, then frames to the context’s rate, interpolating between neighbouring frames. A sound plays as long at any rate: its last frame is held for as long as it lasts.
  3. oto reads the player’s frames from its own goroutine, mixes the players at their volumes and hands them to the system.
  4. The player counts the frames it gave. What is heard now is that count less what still waits in oto’s and the system’s buffers: Player.Position. Just after a loop starts again, what is heard is still the end of the time before.

Films

A film’s pictures play today and its sound does not: Go Renderer’s Android films decode their picture track only, and a browser’s film is muted (browsers play no sound before a touch). The plan:

  • Go Renderer decodes a film’s sound track too, with a second of the phone’s decoders (MediaCodec), and hands its samples out, playing nothing itself.
  • Go UI’s window.Video gives those samples as a stream, and takes a clock: a small interface of its own, so Go UI need not know Go Sound.
  • The sound leads. A picture can be dropped or shown twice and nobody notices; a gap in the sound is always heard. So a player’s Position is the clock, and the film shows the picture due by then.
  • In a browser the film’s own element plays its sound, in step with its pictures; it is unmuted after the first touch.
  • A small package joins the two where both are used (Go UI for Android, or a package here).
